Retry storms are how a small failure becomes a large one. One service gets slow. Callers retry. Retries triple the load. Now the slow service is down and so is everything that depends on it.

We fixed the script in twenty minutes. That's not the point. My point is that I found out on a Tuesday afternoon with no customers affected, instead of a Friday night with everything on fire.

So there's a job now that runs once a month and restores the latest backup into a scratch environment, then runs three checks against it. Row counts, a sample query, and a foreign key.

I ran one last Tuesday and it took nine minutes. Nobody had to log in and nothing broke. Restoring is the skill. Backing up is just copying.

Write a changelog users can read, not a list of commits. I changed mine after a customer replied to a release note asking what had changed for her.

My old entries looked like this. Improved error handling on the upload path. That's true and it means nothing to someone who uploads invoices every day.

The new entry says what's different from their side. If an upload fails now, you get the exact row number that broke instead of a generic message.

Same change. One version got a message thanking me and the other got silence, which I now read as confusion.

I write these in one sitting every two weeks, in the second person, with no internal names. Friday afternoon, twenty minutes, sent before I close the laptop. Your commits are for you. The changelog is for the person paying. I write it for the person who pays, and I read it aloud before I send it.

Study a competitor's product once a quarter, and study the parts that annoy you. I spent an hour in a rival's onboarding flow and came away with two fixes and one idea I threw out.

The fixes were about friction I'd stopped noticing in my own tool. Their empty state explained the next step in four words. Mine had a paragraph I'd written for myself.

The idea I threw out is worth describing. They added a kanban board. It's a good feature for a different product with a different user, and copying it would've cost me three weeks for nothing.

That's the trap of competitor research. You see what they built and assume it works. You can't see how many people use it, which is the question that matters most.

I do that for one hour each quarter. Watch their flow, ignore their feature list, then go read your own empty state. One hour. That's it. It has changed three things in my product this year, and none of them came from their feature list.
