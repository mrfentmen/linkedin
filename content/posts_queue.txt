Retry storms are how a small failure becomes a large one. One service gets slow. Callers retry. Retries triple the load. Now the slow service is down and so is everything that depends on it.

I've watched it twice. The second time I was on call and I could see the request count climbing while the success rate fell, which is the signature you want to recognise.

Two fixes, both small. Retry with a cap and a growing delay between attempts. And add a small random amount of jitter, because a thousand callers all retrying at the one second mark is the same as not backing off at all.

Neither is clever. Both keep the system breathing while something upstream recovers.

I use two caps and one sleep now. Nobody notices it at all. If your retries are immediate and unlimited, you've built a small amplifier pointed at your own weak point.

Batch or stream, the question isn't which is faster. It's which failure you can live with. I got this wrong on a data pipeline and spent a weekend on it.

Streaming gives you numbers that are current and approximate. Batching gives you numbers that are exact and late. Neither is better. They fail in different ways.

My mistake was building a stream for a report that runs every morning. It made the system harder to reason about, and the thing anyone wanted was a correct number at eight in the morning.

I replaced it with a job that runs once a night and takes eleven minutes. Nobody has noticed anything except that the numbers stopped disagreeing with each other.

That's enough. Start with the batch. It's simpler, it's easier to debug, and it's almost always enough. Move to a stream when someone can name a decision that has to happen within seconds.

A queue backlog is invisible until it's an outage. I had one grow for nine days because the consumer was slower than the producer and nobody had a number for either.

The other thing I added was a ceiling. Past ten thousand items, the producer slows down and tells the caller to come back. Losing a request is better than losing nine days of data because the queue filled a disk.

Depth, rate, and a limit. Three numbers on one chart.

A monorepo for one person is the right call. I split mine into three repos for a year and spent that year on version bumps and cross repo commits.

The split made sense when I imagined a team. Each repo had its own pipeline and its own version, and nothing shared. What I got was a dependency I had to publish to myself six times a week.

Moving back took an afternoon and removed two hundred lines of config. One repo, one pipeline, one version. Deploys went from three steps to one.

The case for splitting is real when ownership splits. Different teams, different release cadences, different access. For a solo builder none of that exists, so the cost is pure overhead.

I'd split again at four people. Not before. I moved back in an afternoon and deleted two hundred lines of config. One repo with one pipeline, and no version bumps. One repo. The cost of the split was a version bump six times a week, and that overhead landed on me because there was nobody else to hand it to.

A bad deploy at five in the afternoon gets one decision. Roll back or push forward. I've done both and rolling back has never been the wrong first move.

The instinct to fix forward comes from not wanting to admit the deploy was bad. That's ego and it costs the evening and sometimes the night. Rolling back takes two minutes and returns the product to a known state.

Then you investigate with the pressure off. Twice this year the bug turned out to be something that would have been obvious in the morning, and twice the second deploy was clean.

The one thing I do before rolling back is write a single line in the incident log with the time and the guess. Three words is enough. That line is what makes the postmortem possible an hour later.

Undo first. Think second. Fix third. I roll back first now, and I investigate with the pressure off.

Write tests for the boring paths and skip the clever ones. I've seen a team with eighty percent coverage and no test on the refund flow, because refunds were simple and moving invoices were interesting.

The boring paths are where money and messages move. Login, reset, upload, export, retry, cancel. None of them are fun to test and all of them are the parts a customer notices when they break.

My rule for a new project is five tests before anything else. Sign up, sign in, upload, export, delete. It takes an afternoon and it covers the flows I'd be paged about.

Coverage as a number pushes effort toward whatever is easiest to test, which is not what matters.

I write those five tests before I write a single feature, and it takes one afternoon. They cover the pages I'd be paged about at night. Five tests. One afternoon. Then I build the interesting thing.

A code review comment should describe a problem, not a preference. Comments I've learned from were about something breaking. The comments I've argued with were about taste dressed up as a rule.

My rule now is to say which it is. This will break when the list is empty, or this is a preference and I'll defer to you. That second sentence has ended more review threads than any technical argument I've made.

I separate the two now. My other habit is asking a question instead of writing an instruction. What breaks if the list comes back empty. Half the time the author answers it and the comment was unnecessary. The other half they find the bug.

Reviews are a conversation about a change. Comments that read like verdicts get defended instead of considered, and I've been on both sides of that.

I lead with the kind. That's it. I say which kind of comment it is, and it took two years of arguing about taste before I understood why the two kinds needed different words at all.

Errors get alerts because they're loud. Costs are quiet until the end of the month. I check that number myself once a day.

For a small job, a scheduled script beats a queue. I've set up three message brokers for projects that needed one batch a night, and each one was a week of work I didn't need to do.

A broker earns its place when you need retries, ordering, throughput, and several consumers. For a task that runs once a night and takes eleven minutes, a scheduled script with a log file covers everything you need.

What I do instead is write the job so it's safe to run twice, which is good practice regardless, and have it exit with a clear code. That's the whole infrastructure. The scheduler is already on the machine.

Two of those three brokers are gone. The jobs that use them moved to scripts and nobody noticed, which is the best thing that can happen to infrastructure.

I start every small job that way now. Start with the script. Move when a real constraint appears.

A bug that shows on one device is almost never about the code. Mine showed up on two iPhones and nowhere else, and it took three days to find a date parser that assumed a four digit year.

What I changed wasn't the parser. I made the field accept one format and reject anything else with a message, which turned a silent wrong answer into a visible error.

Write the error message before you write the feature. I've started doing this and it catches half of my bad interface decisions before they exist.

My rule now is that the error text goes in the pull request description next to the code. If I can't write one that helps, the feature isn't finished.

Stopping a project is a decision you should make on a date you chose in advance. I keep a review date next to every new thing, and I hold to it.
