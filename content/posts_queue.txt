Retry storms are how a small failure becomes a large one. One service gets slow. Callers retry. Retries triple the load. Now the slow service is down and so is everything that depends on it.

I've watched it twice. The second time I was on call and I could see the request count climbing while the success rate fell, which is the signature you want to recognise.

Two fixes, both small. Retry with a cap and a growing delay between attempts. And add a small random amount of jitter, because a thousand callers all retrying at the one second mark is the same as not backing off at all.

Neither is clever. Both keep the system breathing while something upstream recovers.

I use two caps and one sleep now. Nobody notices it at all. If your retries are immediate and unlimited, you've built a small amplifier pointed at your own weak point.

Some metrics make people behave worse. I shipped a leaderboard for response time and watched quality fall within a month.

The team got faster. Tickets closed sooner. They also closed without a real answer, because a ticket that looks closed counts, and a ticket waiting on a customer doesn't get you a better number.

I killed it and replaced it with two things. A sample of five closed tickets each week, read by a person. And a customer effort question on the resolution. Slower to read, much harder to game.

Any number attached to a person's performance becomes a target, and targets get optimised whether or not the underlying thing improves.

Measuring is fine. Measuring a person is a different act, and it changes what they do next. I read those five with the person who wrote them. Ten minutes, no scores, no names. Two measures, both read by a person.

Deprecating something is harder than shipping it. I removed an endpoint last month and it took six weeks of finding callers before I could turn anything off.

It had no owner and no docs. Three integrations used it. Two were internal, which I found by grepping. The third was a customer I'd never heard of, and they found out from a log warning I added.

That warning is the whole trick. Before removing anything, log a line every time it's called, with the caller. Run it for a month. Then you know who to talk to instead of guessing.

Removal itself took ten minutes. The finding took six weeks, and it would've been longer with silence.

I add that warning first now, before I write any migration code. One line in a log, left running for a month. That's the whole order. Removal is the last step, not the first.

Hiring your first engineer is a bet on someone's judgement, not their framework knowledge. I got this wrong once and right once, and the difference wasn't skill.

My wrong hire knew more. He could name every design pattern and he rebuilt a working module because he preferred a different approach. Six weeks of churn on something that was fine.

The right hire asked what breaks if we delete this. She shipped smaller diffs, left the working parts alone, and told me when my idea was worse than the current one.

That last part is worth paying for. Someone who'll disagree with the founder in the first month is someone you can trust in the second year.

I ask that question in every interview now. It's never let me down. Never. The answer tells you more than any take home test I've written. I ask about a decision they reversed, never about a framework they prefer.

Saying no to a big customer is a decision you make with your roadmap, not your feelings. I turned down a deal last year that would've doubled my revenue.

The deal wanted a different product. Custom fields, a reporting module, an approval flow. Fine for them, useless to the four hundred people already paying me.

What made it possible to say no was arithmetic. I wrote down the hours it would take and compared them to the hours I had. The number decided it, not my nerve.

Small teams have a fixed number of hours and every yes spends them. Saying yes to a big customer is often saying no to three small ones who already trust you.

The deal found another vendor and did fine. So did I. I'd make the same call again. Revenue grew the following quarter without the deal, and the roadmap stayed pointed at the people who were already paying.

The second version of a product is where founders often ruin the first one. I've been close to doing it. The instinct after a good launch is to rebuild, because now you know how it should've been done.

A rebuild takes four months. It ships with the same bugs in new locations, plus a fresh set nobody's seen. Existing users spend a week relearning things that worked. Some don't come back.

What I do instead is a list. Every change I'd make, ranked by how many people it hurts. Then I ship the top three and stop. That's a third of the value of a rebuild for one week of work.

I've watched two founders do a rebuild and lose half their audience. I don't do it any more. Three items. That's the whole plan. Restraint after success is a real skill and nobody teaches it. The first version earned trust. Don't spend it on a rewrite.

Batch or stream, the question isn't which is faster. It's which failure you can live with. I got this wrong on a data pipeline and spent a weekend on it.

Streaming gives you numbers that are current and approximate. Batching gives you numbers that are exact and late. Neither is better. They fail in different ways.

My mistake was building a stream for a report that runs every morning. It made the system harder to reason about, and the thing anyone wanted was a correct number at eight in the morning.

I replaced it with a job that runs once a night and takes eleven minutes. Nobody has noticed anything except that the numbers stopped disagreeing with each other.

That's enough. Start with the batch. It's simpler, it's easier to debug, and it's almost always enough. Move to a stream when someone can name a decision that has to happen within seconds.

A queue backlog is invisible until it's an outage. I had one grow for nine days because the consumer was slower than the producer and nobody had a number for either.

The fix took an afternoon. I added a check that compares the queue depth to the number of items processed a minute ago, and alerts when the first number is growing for two hours straight. That's the earliest signal there is.

The other thing I added was a ceiling. Past ten thousand items, the producer slows down and tells the caller to come back. Losing a request is better than losing nine days of data because the queue filled a disk.

Depth, rate, and a limit. Three numbers on one chart.

I check that chart when I'm bored now. Depth against rate, on one chart. That's how you find these before a customer does. I look at it for ten seconds a day.

A backup you've never restored isn't a backup. It's a file you hope works. I believed mine worked for two years before I tried one.

The restore failed on the second step. The dump was fine. The restore script referenced a user that no longer existed, and nobody had run it since the migration that removed the user.

We fixed the script in twenty minutes. That's not the point. My point is that I found out on a Tuesday afternoon with no customers affected, instead of a Friday night with everything on fire.

So there's a job now that runs once a month and restores the latest backup into a scratch environment, then runs three checks against it. Row counts, a sample query, and a foreign key.

I ran one last Tuesday and it took nine minutes. Nobody had to log in and nothing broke. Restoring is the skill. Backing up is just copying.

Write a changelog users can read, not a list of commits. I changed mine after a customer replied to a release note asking what had changed for her.

My old entries looked like this. Improved error handling on the upload path. That's true and it means nothing to someone who uploads invoices every day.

The new entry says what's different from their side. If an upload fails now, you get the exact row number that broke instead of a generic message.

Same change. One version got a message thanking me and the other got silence, which I now read as confusion.

I write these in one sitting every two weeks, in the second person, with no internal names. Friday afternoon, twenty minutes, sent before I close the laptop. Your commits are for you. The changelog is for the person paying. I write it for the person who pays, and I read it aloud before I send it.

Study a competitor's product once a quarter, and study the parts that annoy you. I spent an hour in a rival's onboarding flow and came away with two fixes and one idea I threw out.

The fixes were about friction I'd stopped noticing in my own tool. Their empty state explained the next step in four words. Mine had a paragraph I'd written for myself.

The idea I threw out is worth describing. They added a kanban board. It's a good feature for a different product with a different user, and copying it would've cost me three weeks for nothing.

That's the trap of competitor research. You see what they built and assume it works. You can't see how many people use it, which is the question that matters most.

I do that for one hour each quarter. Watch their flow, ignore their feature list, then go read your own empty state. One hour. That's it. It has changed three things in my product this year, and none of them came from their feature list.
