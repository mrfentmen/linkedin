Sleep is the one performance tool that has never failed me. I've tried the timers, the ambient sound, the two monitor setup. None of them moved my output as much as going to bed at the same time for a month.

The difference shows up in the afternoon. Tired, I write more code and understand less of it. I produce volume that has to be reread the next morning, which is negative work with a positive word count.

I measured this badly for years, comparing days by how busy they felt. Busy and productive diverge once you're short on sleep. The busy day is often the one you undo.

So the trade is unglamorous. Less evening output for a better morning. Nobody posts about that. Nearly every bad technical decision I've made came from a depleted brain, and that's a strong enough signal to schedule around. I go to bed first now. Everything else fits around it.

Small teams beat large teams on hard problems and lose on boring ones. That isn't a moral statement, it's arithmetic. Coordination costs grow faster than head count, and the tenth person adds less than the second did.

Where this bites is maintenance. A small team ships a beautiful thing and then has no capacity for the treadmill. Deprecations, certificate renewals, dependency upgrades, the migration that has to happen because a vendor changed a header.

I underestimated that treadmill for years. Every project has one and it takes a steady share of the week regardless of how clever the build is.

So I budget for it now. A line on the plan that says maintenance, separate from features, defended the same way. I've spent three weeks on upgrades this quarter. It never shows up on a feature list. A feature gets applause. The treadmill keeps the lights on, and both of them are the work.

The best code I wrote this year was a deletion. Not a refactor, not a rewrite. I removed a whole subsystem and replaced it with a configuration value.

It existed because two years ago a decision needed to be dynamic. Then the company changed direction and one value was ever used. Four hundred lines, three tests, and a background job nobody monitored, all maintaining a constant.

Deleting it removed a class of incident. That job had failed twice without anyone noticing, because it never mattered.

We talk about building like it's the activity that counts. Noticing that something is no longer needed is rarer and worth more. It takes understanding the whole system well enough to say a piece can go.

Gone in one commit. That's the whole skill. Nobody claps for it. That confidence is the skill. It comes from knowing what the system does at the boundaries, and that takes longer to build than the feature itself ever did. Writing the new code was always the easy part. I still check the boundaries before I trust one.

I keep a file called mistakes. Every entry is a decision I got wrong, one paragraph, no moralising. When I started it I worried it would be discouraging. It's been the opposite.

The pattern is what makes it useful. Five entries about the same mistake in different clothes. Underestimating migrations. Believing a demo is a prototype. Trusting an estimate I made while optimistic.

Numbers don't appear in that file. What appears is the state I was in when I decided. Rushed, tired, hoping a customer would like me. Those are the conditions that produce my bad calls, and they repeat far more reliably than any technical circumstance.

So I have two rules for the conditions I recognise fastest. No architecture decisions after four in the afternoon. No promises in the same meeting where someone is upset. I read the file every quarter now, and I add to it after every project. I check it before I start anything new. That's it. Two rules, and the effect has been enormous.
