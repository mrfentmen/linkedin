Look at churn by where the customer came from. The overall number hides that one source sends you people who leave in a month.

Mine looked healthy at four percent a month. Split by source, the trial from a directory was at eleven and the referrals were at one. Same product, different fit.

The fix wasn't the product. I stopped paying for the directory and put that time into asking happy customers for one introduction each. Total signups fell and revenue rose.

Averages are comfortable because they're one number. The useful view is two columns, source and retention, and it takes an hour to build from data I already had.

Four percent overall. Eleven from one place. I build that table every month now. Two columns. It takes an hour and it has changed where I spend money twice. I'd rather see the split than the total, every single month.

Learn one new thing a quarter and let it be small. Mine this year were a query planner, a background job runner, and a text editor macro. None of them changed my stack.

The value isn't the tool. It's that learning something small teaches me how much I don't know, which keeps me skeptical of confident answers in areas I've never touched.

I timebox it to ten hours across the quarter. If it doesn't click in that window, I stop and write down what I learned, and that note has been more useful than the tool twice.

The alternative is reading about everything and building nothing, which I did for a year and don't recommend.

One thing a quarter. Small, finished, written down. I pick mine in January and I finish it before April every year. Ten hours, then I stop. That's the whole system. Four a year. Forty in a decade.

Saying no to yourself is the hard version. I have eleven side projects and two of them get time, and the other nine are a tax on my attention every time I open the folder.

What I changed was writing a status on each one. Active, paused, or dead. Nine are dead, and I stopped feeling like I owed them anything.

The folder is still there. What went away was the guilt, which was the actual cost, because a paused project sits in your head and takes a slice of every evening.

Now I add a new one if something dies first. The rule is brutal and it's kept me from starting four things this year, which is the point.

Eleven projects. Two alive. I wrote a status on every project and closed nine of them. The folder is still there and the guilt is gone. Say no to yourself first.
