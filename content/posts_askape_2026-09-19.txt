I work on an AI product that people use to research their own money, and it changed how I write code.

Not because the code is harder. Because the consequences are. A bug in a game is annoying. A wrong number next to someone's portfolio becomes a decision they make badly, and they won't know it was me.

So AskApe has one rule that shapes everything we ship. Never fake certainty. That rule is harder to build than any feature. A disclaimer at the bottom of the page costs nothing and fixes nothing. What works is showing where a number came from, what period it covers, and what it leaves out.

I've written plenty of features that were fun. This is the first one where being vague is the bug itself.

I learned more about finance in 6 months here than in all the years I spent reading about it, because I can't hand wave anything. Someone will ask why the number is what it is.

The hardest question I get about my job is what the product does.

People hear AI and finance in the same sentence and assume we predict stocks. We don't. AskApe is a research tool. You ask it a question in plain English about a company, a portfolio, or a filing, and it pulls the pieces together and shows you where each one came from.

That sounds like a small thing until you've done the alternative. I've spent an evening working out what a company earns after one time charges, across 3 tabs, 2 PDFs, and a search result from 2021. The tool does that part in seconds. The judgement stays mine, which is where I want it.

What we don't do matters as much. We don't take custody of anyone's money. We don't place trades on our own. We don't tell you what to buy. Every one of those limits is deliberate, and they're the reason I'm comfortable working here.

I've been in products where the pitch was bigger than the thing. This one is the other way round.

The tools that people need for this stuff have always existed. They just cost more than a car.

When I started here I assumed the job was making a cheaper version of a terminal. It isn't. It's making the same information usable by somebody who doesn't have 3 hours and a finance degree to spare. Those are different problems and the second one is much harder.

A professional can read a dense table and know within 10 seconds which numbers matter, because they have seen a thousand tables like it. Everybody else sees 40 numbers and no idea which 2 are the story. So most of the work is not fetching data. It's deciding what to leave out and what to explain in the same breath.

I find that part interesting. Anyone can render a table. Explaining why the number moved, and what it doesn't tell you, is the product.

I don't think retail investors are less serious. They have less time. Good tools respect that.

Every feature request we get can be traced back to a sentence somebody didn't understand.

Not a missing number. A missing explanation. So we spend a lot of time on plain English, and I mean that as an engineering constraint rather than a tone of voice. If a screen needs a glossary, it's broken.

The test I use is whether my dad would get it. He's not stupid, he just doesn't work in finance, and he's not going to look up "adjusted" before deciding whether he cares. If the answer depends on him knowing one industry word, I've shipped a bad answer.

I'm still surprised by how much harder simple is. Jargon is a shortcut. It assumes you already know. Writing "the company made less per sale than last quarter" takes 4 more drafts than "margin compression".

I have never once looked at a finance tool and wished it were more technical. Not once.

A demo of an AI finance tool and the thing you'd trust with your portfolio are about 90 percent different work.

I learned that here. Making a model answer a question is a weekend. Making it answer the same question the same way, with the right source attached, at 8:30 in the morning on a heavy news day, is the job. Demos don't have bad days. Products do.

So I spend my time on the unglamorous half. What happens when a provider is slow. What happens when a filing lands 10 minutes late. What the screen looks like when we have nothing to say, because that's a real answer and it shouldn't look like an error.

I don't post about that work. It's also the reason the impressive part is worth anything.

I've shipped features that looked great in a demo and I'd rather not do it again. Trust is built in the boring cases.

I learned more about finance from writing software for it than from any book I tried to read.

Books explain the system. Code explains the edge cases. I know how a dividend works. I did not know that a company's fiscal year isn't the calendar year until a date range broke, and I did not know how messy "reported" versus "adjusted" gets until I had to show both on one line.

Every one of those lessons showed up as a bug first. That's the most expensive way to learn anything and it's also the only way that stuck.

So I stopped trying to study my way into the domain. I read the filings for the companies users ask about most, and I pay attention when a number surprises me. Curiosity that has a real question behind it beats a curriculum.

I'm still not an expert. I'm just a developer who now flinches at 3 specific kinds of wrong number, and that's more useful than general knowledge.

The most valuable thing I've worked on here is a connection that just doesn't break, and I can't show anyone a screenshot of it.

Nobody signs up for reliability. They sign up for the thing it enables and they notice when it's gone. Brokerage connections are the least glamorous surface in this product and the one I think about most, because everything else depends on the holdings being right.

So I look at the failures instead of the features. What happens when a provider revokes access. What happens when a sync lands halfway. What a user sees when their positions are 2 days stale, because showing stale data without saying so is worse than showing nothing.

I argued about that one for 3 weeks.

I've made peace with the fact that my best work here will never demo well. Reliability is invisible when it works, and the reward is that nobody has a reason to think about it. That is the job.

I stopped being impressed by fast answers and started asking where they came from.

Any product can produce a confident sentence about a stock. That takes an API call and an afternoon. What's hard is producing the sentence and the receipt, so you can check it and disagree with it. The receipt is the product.

I changed what I build because of it. When a number shows up on a screen here, there's a line under it that says what period it covers and what it excludes, and a way to get to the source. That's 3 small UI decisions and about 8 weeks of backend work that nobody will ever tweet about.

The reason it matters is simple. If you can't trace a number, you can't trust it, and if you can't trust it you're just reading a horoscope with a stock ticker attached.

Confidence is cheap now. Receipts don't demo well. Provenance is the scarce thing, and it's the part I'd defend hardest if someone tried to cut it for speed.

The reason I took this job is that the person on the other end of it is getting a worse deal than the people already in the building.

That sounds dramatic. It isn't. Research quality is a function of time and access. Most people have neither. So they get their information from a headline, a comment section, or a creator who is paid to be certain. Then they make a real decision with real money.

I don't think software fixes that. I do think it makes a dent, and a dent is worth a lot when the alternative is nothing. Giving somebody the same numbers a professional gets, in language they can use, is a decent thing to spend 8 hours on.

The bar I hold myself to is whether I'd be comfortable with my own family using it. That's a low bar and it's also a stricter one than any spec I've been given.

It's the first job where the mission didn't make me roll my eyes.
