I changed the window and the threshold together. Pages went from about nine a month to one. Nine to one. That page was real. It was the first real one in seven weeks and people answered it.

The fix took twenty minutes. I spent the real time on the judgment afterward. Tune an alert too hard and it goes quiet, and it takes its coverage with it. Leave it noisy and it takes the whole channel down.

A false positive is a design bug, not a fact about the world. Fix the alert or delete it. Don't teach people to ignore the channel.

I wrote a post about a mistake I made and four people told me privately that they'd made the same one. Four messages. Likes were few. That told me more than the reactions did.

That's the case for writing about failure instead of wins. A win gets a like and a mistake gets a message, and messages are where the real exchange happens. Nobody messages you to say they did something right.

It costs you specificity. A vague post about learning a lot gets nothing. A post that says I dropped a table in staging and here's the check I added gets three people telling you their version.

I write the mistake with the detail in it now. Detail is what makes it usable to somebody else, and it's also what makes it readable. Everybody can smell a summary. That's the point. Four messages is a small number and it's the one I remember.

I counted how long a review sat before somebody opened it. Average was about eleven hours. Eleven hours. The fix wasn't to nag people. It was to make the pull request smaller.

I watched a four hundred line change wait most of a day because nobody had forty minutes. Forty minutes. Nobody had that much to spare. A forty line change got read in twenty minutes and the review was better. The reviewer could hold the whole thing in their head.

I set a soft limit of two hundred lines and a rule that a big change gets split with a note about the order to read it in. Median wait dropped to two hours inside a month. Two hours.

Reviews aren't slow because people are busy. They're slow because the work is too big to start. A big change isn't reviewed slower. It isn't reviewed at all. Shrink the change and the wait takes care of itself.

I met somebody who had been at the same company for eleven years. I asked how he kept learning. He changed teams on purpose every two years and stayed because the problems were real. Eleven years. I'd dismissed that strategy for a long time.

He'd been through a migration, an acquisition, and a rewrite, and each time he was the person who knew where the old assumptions were written down. That knowledge was worth more than any single system.

The trade is that you don't get the title bumps as fast and recruiters ignore you. He said the second one was a feature. The first one he stopped caring about at year six.

Staying can be a strategy and not a lack of ambition. Every two years. Depth compounds in one place. It doesn't travel well. Both of those things are true at once. Eleven years at one company and he wasn't stuck.
