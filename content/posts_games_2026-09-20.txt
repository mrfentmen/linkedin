The most common argument about game sales doesn't survive contact with the market size. Here's the version everyone uses. This old game sold fourteen million and this new game sold forty million, so the new one is bigger. That treats the market as fixed.

It wasn't. The global games market was about forty five billion dollars in 2007. In 2023 it was closer to a hundred and eighty four billion. That's about four times the spending from about four times the buyers.

So adjust it. Fourteen million in a forty five billion dollar market isn't four hundred million in a hundred and eighty four billion dollar market. It's a bigger share of a much smaller pond.

I used to make this mistake constantly. I'd pull two years of numbers and compare them like the world stood still in between. It doesn't. I still see people do it. Phones happened. Free to play happened. Half of that growth is mobile, which is a different audience buying a different thing. The honest answer is that I don't know how to compare 2007 to 2023 fairly, and neither does anyone else arguing about it. Which means the argument can't decide anything.

A remaster has one job. Lose nothing.

The good ones add a resolution bump, some quality of life, and get out of the way. Old audio stays. Old art stays. Nobody gets a vote on what the old version should have been.

I play remasters for the old version, not the new one. I want the thing I remember, cleaned up. That's the whole product. If I wanted a remake I'd buy a remake. So when a remaster drops character options or deletes armor sets from 2006, it isn't a creative decision. It's a decision made by someone who thinks the old thing was wrong, shipped inside a product whose only selling point is the old thing.

That's the contradiction I can't get past. The pitch is nostalgia. The delivery is editing. I'd respect it more if it were a remake, because then the argument is creative and I can disagree with it. Calling it a remaster and changing the content is a way to skip the argument. Ship the old version. Charge for it.

I stopped arguing about games with other people's sales numbers.

For a while I had a habit. Someone would say a design choice hurt a game, and I'd go find a chart. Two million copies, top of the chart, argument over. I thought I was being rigorous. I was being lazy. Sales measure how many people bought something. They don't measure whether it was any good.

Treating them as the same thing is how you end up defending a game you don't like because the spreadsheet says you should.

I still use sales for one thing, and only one. Working out what a company will do next. That's a forecast, not a verdict. If you want to argue a game got worse, name the change and explain why it was worse. That's a harder argument. It's also the only one that survives someone disagreeing with you.

The tool is almost never the problem. The motive is.

I build games with AI myself. It's the most fun I've had making anything, and I'll talk about it all day. So I'm not the guy telling you AI ruins games. Here's what I do think. Watch what a studio uses it for. If they use it to build something that was impossible before, you can feel it in the first hour. If they use it to avoid paying artists, you feel that faster.

Same technology. Opposite outcome. The difference is whether anyone in the building wanted the game to be good or wanted the budget line to go down. I've watched this pattern in software for years now. Teams that use AI to remove people ship worse products and lose the experienced people who knew why the old decisions were made. Teams that use it to let people do more keep everyone and go faster. Mine went from 340 dollars a month to 11 by routing tasks to smaller models, and nobody got let go.

That isn't a technology question. It's a management question wearing a technology costume. So I've stopped asking whether a studio uses AI. I ask what they cut, and who decided.

I built a game this year and the hardest part was deciding what to leave out.

Everyone assumes the hard part is the code. It isn't. I write code with AI and it goes fast. The bottleneck moved somewhere I didn't expect, which is taste. When you can build almost anything, the question stops being can I make this. It becomes should this be in the game. Every feature costs you the thing you didn't add instead, and nobody teaches you how to price that.

I cut a whole combat system three weeks in. It worked. It was fine. It made the game worse by being in there, because it pulled attention away from the part that was good.

I've never made a decision like that at a job. At a job you add things, because adding is visible and cutting needs a reason that survives a status meeting. That's the argument for building alone, and it isn't about speed. It's that the only person I have to convince is me, and I'm allowed to be wrong quickly. Most design problems are subtraction problems. Most companies are built to reward addition.
